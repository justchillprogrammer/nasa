    The sky is not as still as it looks.

    Scroll, and watch a real star move.

    Every six months, NASA’s SPHEREx telescope photographs the entire sky in infrared.

    This is one small patch of it: a quarter of a degree across, half the width of the full Moon. Every speck is a star.

    In 1930, Clyde Tombaugh found Pluto by flipping between two photographs of the same stars.

    He used a blink comparator. Whatever jumped had moved. Keep scrolling to flip these two pictures, taken a year apart.

    Sky Blink does the same with SPHEREx, for any patch of sky.

    It lines up every pass, finds every star, and works out which ones moved or changed brightness.

    This is Barnard’s Star.

    It crosses our sky faster than any other star. Your scroll is the calendar.

    How Sky Blink finds a change

    1. Stack 30 to 40 exposures from each pass into one clean picture.
    2. Match every star between passes. Anything that moved in a straight line, or got brighter or fainter, stands out from the noise.
    3. Check for false alarms: reflections inside the telescope, glare from bright stars, glowing gas clouds.
    4. Explain each result in plain language. Every number in the text is checked against the measurements.

    What changed here

      How Sky Blink finds changes

      For each six-monthly sweep, Sky Blink stacks the 30 to 40 SPHEREx exposures taken between 1 and 2 micrometres into one clean image. It finds every star, matches them between sweeps, and keeps only changes far larger than the measurement noise.

      A brightness change is then checked again in the single exposures, comparing the star only at matching wavelengths and against nearby ordinary stars. Changes that pass are marked confirmed; the rest are shown as candidates.

      Very bright stars overload the detector; their centres carry no data and are drawn white.

      The plain-language text is written by an AI from these measurements only, and every number it uses is checked against them. Images shown between two sweeps are blends, not new observations.

      Images: NASA SPHEREx Level 2 spectral images from the NASA/IPAC Infrared Science Archive. Detections are automated measurements and can be wrong; candidates are labelled as such.
